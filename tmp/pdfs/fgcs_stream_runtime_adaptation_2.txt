Abstract
Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) consist of autonomous entities that operate individually or collaboratively to complete tasks, relying
on coordination mechanisms to assign responsibilities, manage failures, and regulate agent interactions. Since coordination deter-
mines how tasks are allocated, transferred, and recovered during execution, it directly affects the MAS performance objective, such
as reducing task latency or improving task completion under uncertain operating conditions. Yet, coordination mechanisms are
selected at design time and kept fixed during execution. This limits their effectiveness when workload, agent failures, communica-
tion losses, or malicious behaviors change at runtime. This paper addresses this limitation by framing the selection of coordination
mechanisms as a runtime adaptation problem. We propose a dynamic adaptation strategy that combines stream-based runtime mon-
itoring and online learning. RTLola, a stream-based monitoring language formalism, computes prespecified MAS performance
objectives from the simulation event stream, while a contextual bandit uses this monitored feedback together with the current op-
erating context to select among three representative coordination mechanisms. We implement the dynamic adaptation strategy in
COORDINATE 2.0, a discrete-event simulation tool for configuring, executing, and analyzing fault-tolerant MAS scenarios. The
evaluation, conducted in a smart-hospital scenario, shows that monitoring-guided adaptation can improve MAS performance under
changing workload and fault conditions, while introducing negligible runtime overhead.
Keywords:Multi-Agent Systems, Stream-based Monitoring, Online Learning, Contextual Bandit, Performance Evaluation
1. Introduction
Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) are networks of autonomous
software or hardware entities, called agents, designed to interact
and collaborate to complete a common task [31, 20]. MAS are
used in domains such as healthcare, logistics, and smart man-
ufacturing, where agents operate in dynamic physical environ-
ments and contribute to system-level goals through individual
or collaborative tasks (e.g.,a mobile robot delivering medical
items in a hospital). Although each agent pursues its own local
tasks, the system evaluates their joint behavior throughperfor-
mance objectives, which aggregate task outcomes and capture
overall MAS performance (e.g.,On average, how many items
are successfully delivered per hour?). Achieving these objec-
tives requires coordination, since agents must decide how tasks
are assigned, transferred, and recovered when failures or delays
occur. Consequently, coordination mechanisms such as central-
ized schedulers or fully decentralized policies play a key role in
ensuring that tasks are completed while meeting quality or time
constraints (e.g.,Patient X should receive the medicine within
10 minutes.) [22, 25].
Yet, classical design-time practices typically fix a single co-
ordination mechanisma priori, based on offline evaluations of

To address this limitation, we treat the runtime selection of

key idea is to close the loop between stream-based runtime

current MAS performance objective and operational conditions.

the end of each window,COORDINATE 2.0combines the mon-

operating conditions, such as workload, failures, communica-

2. Related Work
In the following, we review the key aspects of MAS along the
four dimensions considered in this work: coordination mecha-
nisms, discrete-event simulation tools, stream-based monitor-
ing, and online learning.
2.1. Coordination Mechanisms for MAS
Many recent studies have focused on coordination mecha-
nisms, which are a fundamental aspect of modern systems and a
key component of MAS [35]. Rizk et al. [26] survey coordina-
tion in MAS, highlighting Markov decision processes, swarm
intelligence, and graph-theoretic frameworks. Swarm intelli-
gence algorithms are used in [27] for autonomous coordination
in large-scale MAS, while Yun et al. [33] introduce a leader-
based mechanism to improve data resolution and area cover-
age for aerial vehicles; a related problem of reducing delivery
time while prioritizing urgent tasks under collision avoidance
constraints is tackled in [34]. Beyond new mechanisms, sev-
eral works quantify MAS performance under varying assump-
tions: task completion time and efficiency are studied in [17]
via reward-based task offloading, Stochastic Petri Nets [18] en-
able performance analysis but suffer from state-space explo-
sion, and the stochastic models in [24] address coordination in
constrained settings, without explicitly considering reliability.
2.2. Simulation Tools for MAS
Discrete-event simulators for MAS are tools for modeling
and analyzing complex environments in which multiple au-
tonomous entities (i.e., agents) interact with one another and
their surroundings, with state changes occurring at discrete
points in time. These tools are usually used to study complex
emergent behaviors and complex dynamics arising from the in-
teractions of multiple agents that follow predefined rules [27].
In the literature, several simulators support the design and
simulation of MAS. ARGoS [23] provides swarm robotics sce-
narios that showcase properties such as synchronization, flock-
ing, and gripping. Gazebo [13], combined with the Robotics
System Toolbox [19], targets robots operating in complex en-
vironments and supports detailed kinematic and dynamic mod-
eling, motion planning, and trajectory generation. However, to
the best of our knowledge, none of the MAS simulators cur-
rently support: (i) the implementation and evaluation of alter-
native coordination mechanisms among agents; (ii) the contin-
uous and dynamic monitoring of MAS performance objectives
during execution; and (iii) the runtime adaptation of coordina-
tion mechanisms based on monitored performance indicators
and evolving operating conditions.
2.3. Stream-Based Monitoring
Monitoring of real-time metrics is often based on tempo-
ral logics such as STL [16] and its variants, which provide
boolean and quantitative (robustness-based) verdicts [7]. How-
ever, these formalisms struggle with expressing aggregation and
filtering, prompting several extensions [30, 5, 29], and their
syntactic restrictions [1] limit the manipulation of real-valued
signals and the specification of many CPS properties. Stream-
based runtime monitoring has therefore emerged as a practi-
cal alternative for systems that require continuous quantitative
feedback, supporting both signal- and point-based semantics.
A prominent example is RTLola[2], a real-time stream spec-
ification language with asynchronous input streams, sliding-
window aggregations, and static resource analysis. It has been

used in domains such as autonomous aircraft [2], where speci-

2.4. Online Learning
Online learning is a machine learning paradigm designed for
settings where data are not available all at once but arrive pro-
gressively over time [28]. Instead of training a model once on
a fixed dataset, as in offline machine learning, online learning
allows the model to continue training incrementally as new data
become available [12]. This makes online learning particularly
useful in dynamic environments, where predictions or decisions
must be made while the observed context may change over
time. In these settings, the model cannot rely solely on knowl-
edge learned before deployment; it must progressively adapt its
predictive or decision-making behavior as new observations re-
flect changes in the evolving context [12].
Contextual bandits are an online learning approach for prob-
lems in which decisions must be made repeatedly under chang-
ing conditions [14]. At each decision step, the algorithm ob-
serves the current situation, called thecontext, and uses it to
choose one option from the availableactions. After the action
is applied, the algorithm receives feedback, called areward, in-
dicating how effective the choice was. Since only the selected
action is applied, the algorithm observes feedback only for that
action, and not for the alternatives that were not selected. Over
time, it uses the collected rewards to improve the model that
associates contexts with actions.
This makes contextual bandits suitable for our dynamic adap-
tation problem, where the system must repeatedly select a co-
ordination mechanism under changing runtime conditions. In
our setting, the context vector includes the monitored value of
the selected MAS performance objective, along with variables
describing current operating conditions, such as workload, fail-
ures, communication degradation, and malicious behavior. The
actions correspond to the coordination mechanisms that can be
selected during execution, while the reward is derived from the
MAS performance objective’s value observed after applying the
chosen mechanism. RTLolaprovides the monitoring layer by
computing the selected performance objective from the simula-
tion event stream. The contextual bandit provides the learning
layer by using the context vector and the observed reward to
improve future adaptation decisions.

Topology.COORDINATE 2.0allows users to build their own
physical space of interest by specifying rooms and their connec-

ones, representing the paths the agents can take. Figure 1 shows
seven standard topologies from the literature [9] that are imple-
mented inCOORDINATE 2.0:Ring, where the chain is closed
into a cycle;Fully-Connected, where each room is directly con-
nected to all others;Star, where all rooms connect to a central
hub;Mesh, where rooms is connected to a subset of multiple
rooms that in turn are connected to other nodes;Line, where
rooms form a simple chain;Tree, where rooms are organized
hierarchically; andBus, where rooms are connected through a
central corridor. It is worth noting thatCOORDINATE 2.0also
supports the design ofcustomtopologies, allowing practitioners
to manually specify the number of rooms and their connections
through the tool’s GUI.
Task.A task represents a system goal to be achieved by the
agents. InCOORDINATE 2.0, a task models the generation of
an item that must be collected from a source room and deliv-
ered to a destination room by an agent. Tasks are generated
during the simulation based on two configurable parameters:
the interarrival time and the event arrival distribution. The in-
terarrival time defines the expected time between consecutive
tasks, while the event arrival distribution (i.e., uniform, expo-
nential, or deterministic) specifies how this timing is sampled.
Together, these parameters control the rate at which workload
is injected into the system. Each item is characterized by an im-
portance level (e.g., Low, Medium, or High), a source location,
and a destination. The task priority represents the urgency of
completing the task. The simulator allows assigning a spawn
probability to each priority level as a percentage, enabling con-
trol over how frequently low-, medium-, and high-priority tasks
are generated during the simulation. For each task, both the
source and destination rooms can be configured as either fixed

4. Dynamic Adaptation
This section presents the proposed Dynamic Adaptation
(DA) strategy for selecting coordination mechanisms at runtime
in fault-tolerant MAS. DA frames coordination selection as an
online decision problem: during execution, the system collects
runtime indicators, uses them to characterize the current oper-
ating context, and relies on a contextual bandit to select and
progressively refine the choice among CE, SD, and FD based
on the observed performance.
The following subsections first describe the overall adapta-
tion loop, then the monitoring layer based on RTLola, and fi-
nally the learning layer, where a contextual bandit model sug-
gests the coordination mechanism to apply.

During simulation,COORDINATE 2.0continuously gener-
ates a timestamped event stream that records events in the simu-
lated environment. This stream captures the system’s evolution
over time and serves as input to the Monitor Layer. The Moni-
tor Layer, implemented in RTLola, consumes the event stream
during each monitoring windowW j and derives a set of run-
time key indicators from the observed events. These indicators
translate the MAS performance objectives into observable mea-
sures, making it possible to assess how well each objective is
being satisfied in the current operating conditions. At the end of
each monitoring windowW j,COORDINATE 2.0combines the
performance indicators produced by RTLolawith MAS sys-
tem parameters to build the context vectorx j, which is then
provided to the Learning Layer. The Learning Layer, imple-
mented as a contextual bandit, usesx j as input to its learning
model to estimate which coordination mechanism is expected
to perform best in the current context. Based on this estimate,
it selects a coordination mechanismc j from the available alter-
natives{CE,SD,FD}.
Once the following window has been observed, the Learning
Layer computes the rewardr j+1 from the experienced perfor-
mance and updates its model using it. The reward, therefore,
arrives with a one-window delay, reflecting the performance
actually experienced under the chosen mechanism. When a
switch is triggered,COORDINATE 2.0applies the new coordi-
nation mechanism without restarting the simulation.
4.2. Monitoring Layer
The monitoring layer is implemented through RTLola, in-
troduced in Section 2.3. During simulation, RTLolaanalyzes
in real time the trace generated byCOORDINATE 2.0and ap-

MAS performance objectives. These objectives are not fixed
by the adaptation mechanism: practitioners can define one or
more of them directly in the RTLolaspecification, using de-

produced by the simulator. In this way, the monitored feedback
is customizable, while the adaptation logic remains unchanged.
Worth to note that, in this work, we define theRelative Task

specification focuses on Createevents, which mark the gen-

RTLolareconstructs the lifecycle of each delivered item and

while RTLolaprovides formal guarantees on the structure and

4.3. Learning Layer
The Learning Layer implements the contextual bandit model
described in Section 2.4. At the end of each monitoring win-
dowW j, it receives the context vectorx j, which summarizes
the current operating conditions of the MAS. The context com-
bines the monitored MAS performance objective produced by
the Monitor Layer with a subset of the simulation metrics intro-
duced in Section 3.3, organized into task processing, fault and
communication behavior, and scenario and resource usage.
In the current implementation, the task-related part of the
context captures whether the MAS is keeping up with the gener-
ated workload; the fault and communication part captures run-
time degradation due to failures, message losses, and deadline
misses; and the scenario and resource part captures the avail-
able agent capacity, the fraction of malicious agents, the con-
figured fault conditions, and the currently active coordination
mechanism. These values serve as contextual information for
the bandit, along with theRelative Task LatencyMAS perfor-
mance objective. The action space of the bandit is the set of
coordination mechanisms supported byCOORDINATE 2.0:
Mcoord ={FD,SD,CE}.
Givenx j, the bandit evaluates the mechanisms inM coord and
selects the mechanismc j with the highest expected reward in
the current context. The selected mechanism is then communi-
cated toCOORDINATE 2.0, which applies it without restarting
the simulation or modifying the current simulation state.
Learning is driven by delayed feedback. Aftercj has been ap-
plied during the following monitoring windowWj+1, the Learn-
ing Layer computes the rewardr j+1 from the observed perfor-
mance. Higher reward values correspond to better satisfaction
of the monitored MAS performance objective (i.e., reducing
Relative Task Latency). The model is then updated using the
tuple (xj,c j,r j+1).
5. Experiments Design
5.1. Research Questions
We aim to address the following research questions:
•RQ1. Does Dynamic Adaptation improve the selected
MAS performance objective compared with fixed co-
ordination mechanisms?We evaluate whether the pro-
posed DA strategy improves the MAS’s performance ob-
jective by adapting the coordination mechanism at runtime
rather than keeping it fixed throughout the simulation.
•RQ2. How does Dynamic Adaptation react to changing
workload and fault conditions during simulation?We
relate DA coordination switches to the monitored value of
the MAS performance objective, the context variables, and
the dynamic fault windows.
•RQ3. What is the runtime overhead introduced by Dy-
namic Adaptation?We measure the additional cost in-
troduced by DA by considering both the monitoring and
learning layers.
•RQ4. How doesCOORDINATE 2.0scale across demand-
ing MAS simulation settings and different hardware
profiles?We evaluate the tool’s cost across different simu-
lation settings and hardware profiles to understand the ex-
tent to which it can be realistically used.
5.2. Experimental Scenario
Inspired by the Ghent University smart hospital [21], we con-
sider a hospital logistics scenario in which autonomous agents,
modeled as mobile robots, support clinical staffby transporting
medical items across the hospital. InCOORDINATE 2.0, each
MAS task corresponds to the delivery of a single item, repre-
senting a medical request such as a pill or medical equipment.
Items are generated at a source location (e.g., a pharmacy or
storage area) and must be delivered to target rooms (e.g., patient
rooms, wards, or nursing stations). The hospital topology is
modeled as a graph of interconnected rooms, where nodes rep-
resent rooms and edges represent traversable paths. As shown
in Figure 7, this topology captures the spatial constraints that
shape agent mobility, delivery times, and recovery operations.
The primary performance objective is to minimizeTask Rela-
tive Latency, i.e., the delay between the generation of a delivery
request and the successful delivery of the corresponding item to
its target room. This objective is relevant in hospital logistics
because delays in delivering medicines or medical equipment
may disrupt clinical workflows or affect patient safety [21, 25].
Effective coordination mechanisms are therefore needed to al-
locate tasks, react to failures, and recover unfinished deliveries.
At the same time, hospital operating conditions are inherently
dynamic: workload may increase during busy periods, agents
may fail while carrying items, communication may be delayed
or lost, and malicious agents may issue misleading coordination
requests. Under these conditions, a mechanism that is effec-
tive in one operating context may become less suitable later. A
static approach cannot react to this shift, whereas DA monitors
the system at runtime and switches the coordination mechanism
when the observed performance indicates that the current one is
no longer appropriate.

5.4. Dynamic Fault Windows
To instantiate dynamic fault windows, we apply four fault
windows over each simulated day, as reported in Table 4. Each
window temporarily changes four input parameters from Ta-
ble 3: the task inter-arrival timeT spawn, the number of mali-
cious agentsN malicious, the agent failure probabilityF agent, and
the message loss probabilityF msg. The values in the table are
expressed as percentage variations with respect to the corre-
sponding baseline values, while≡indicates that the baseline
value is kept unchanged. During execution,COORDINATE 2.0
selects the active window according to the current time within
the simulated day. The values reported in Table 4 replace the
corresponding baseline values only while that window is active.
When the window ends, the simulation continues with the next
one, without restarting the run or resetting the system state.
The first window, from 0 to 6 h, models more favorable con-
ditions by reducing fault intensity and workload:T spawn in-
creases by 50%, so tasks are generated less frequently, while
Nmalicious,F agent, andF msg decrease. The second window, from
6 to 10 h, increases the workload by reducingT spawn by 25%
and increases the agent failure probability by 20%, while keep-
ing malicious agents and message loss unchanged. The third
window, from 10 to 14 h, keeps all four parameters equal to
their baseline values. The fourth window, from 14 to 18 h, in-
troduces a more degraded condition by increasing workload,
malicious agents, agent failures, and message loss. Finally, the
last window, from 18 to 24 h, represents a recovery period, with
lower workload and reduced fault-related parameters.

6. Evaluation
6.1. RQ1: Effectiveness of Dynamic Adaptation
RQ1 evaluates whether DA improves the MAS performance
objective relative to fixed coordination mechanisms. The anal-
ysis compares DA with CE, SD, and FD across the same exper-
imental campaign, varying workload intensity, the number of
agents, the malicious-agent ratio, and agent fault probability.
Table 5 aggregates the results by coordination mechanism:
for each mechanism, each metric is averaged over all work-
load intensities, number of agents, malicious-agent ratios, fault
probabilities, and repetitions. Results at different levels of gran-
ularity are available in the replication package [6]. The evalu-
ation compares the coordination mechanisms across four met-
rics:Average latencyis the arithmetic mean of the time be-
tween task creation and task delivery, computed over delivered
tasks; it can increase when a small subset of delivered tasks is
completed very late.Median latencyis computed on the same
delivered-task latencies after sorting them: 50% of delivered
tasks are completed no later than this value, and 50% are com-
pleted no earlier than this value.Completion ratereports the
fraction of generated tasks that are successfully delivered. We
also report the average number ofdeliveredandexpiredtasks
per run, rounded to the nearest task. Finally, the table reports
completion-latency efficiency, computed for each run as the ra-
tio of the completion rate to the median latency, and then aver-
aged across all runs.
Results in Table 5 show that FD reports the lowestaverage
latency, equal to 1.66 h, together with the lowestcompletion
rate, equal to 7.66%. FD also reports 1489 delivered tasks and
18852 expired tasks. SD reports 1.83 h average latency, 8.12
min median latency, 19.59% completion rate, 3961 delivered
tasks, and 16413 expired tasks. CE reports the highest comple-
tion rate, with 24.12%, the highest number of delivered tasks,
with 4826, and the lowest number of expired tasks, with 15557;
its average latency is 2.19 h, and its median latency is 11.09
min. DA reports 1.87 h average latency, 8.09 min median la-
tency, 19.43% completion rate, 3929 delivered tasks, and 16449
expired tasks. The completion-latency efficiency is highest for
DA, with 11.27, followed by SD with 9.77, CE with 3.77, and
FD with 3.14.

lowest completion rate.
6.2. RQ2: Dynamic Adaptation Implications
RQ2 examines when DA changes the active coordination
mechanism and which runtime conditions are associated with
these changes. Unlike RQ1, which compares DA with fixed co-
ordination mechanisms, RQ2 focuses only on DA runs under
dynamic operating profiles.
Each adaptation check logs the monitored runtime context,
the computed performance state, and the selected coordina-
tion mechanism. The performance state is computed from
latency, pending-task pressure, failure rate, and message-loss
rate. In addition, DA logs two contextual flags:recovery mode,
which identifies low-fault and low-communication-loss condi-
tions, andFD pressure, which identifies conditions associated
with communication degradation, malicious-agent pressure, or
high failure pressure. A switch is recorded when the selected
mechanism differs from the currently active one.
Table 6 reports the runtime contexts observed at switch time.
Recovery mode accounts for 900 switches, all selecting CE.
FD pressure accounts for 1310 switches, all selecting FD, with
higher median failure rate and non-zero median message-loss
rate. Degraded or critical states without FD pressure account
for 1063 switches, all selecting SD. Stable states account for 47
switches, all selecting CE.
Figure 10 shows that switches are distributed differently
across dynamic profiles and phases. In nominal phases,
switches select SD in most cases, with a small fraction selecting
CE. Communication-stress and malicious-stress phases select
FD almost exclusively. Load/failure-stress phases mostly se-
lect FD, with a smaller fraction selecting SD. Recovery phases
select CE.

6.3. RQ3: Runtime Overhead
RQ3 analyzes the runtime cost of enabling DA relative to
fixed coordination mechanisms. The goal is to quantify the
additional execution cost introduced by the DA’s stream-based
monitoring and online adaptation pipeline. We therefore com-
pare CE, SD, FD, and DA under the same scenario, varying the
workload intensity (T spawn ∈ {150,120,90}seconds) and the
number of agents (N agent ∈{10,20,30}). Each configuration is
repeated 10×, for a total of 4×3×3×10=360 simulation runs.
We assess the DA runtime overhead using two indicators.
Simulation wall timemeasures the real time required to com-
plete a run and captures the end-to-end cost of executing each
mechanism, andAdaptation overhead per check, which isolates
the direct cost of the DA adaptation pipeline, including moni-
tor queries, adaptation decisions, and adaptation-state updates
at each adaptation interval.
Figure 12 reports the average overhead of each DA adap-
tation check. The overhead remains low across the evaluated
settings, ranging from 0.10 to 1.17 ms depending on workload
intensity and fleet size. The cost increases mainly with the num-
ber of agents, since larger agent populations generate more run-
time information to process during monitoring and adaptation.
Across all DA runs, the average adaptation check requires 0.46
ms. This cost is mostly due to the RTLolaquery, which ac-
counts for 0.42 ms on average, i.e., more than 90% of the mea-
sured per-check cost. The learning and scoring step accounts
for only 0.03 ms on average, showing that the decision logic
itself is not the dominant source of overhead.
Table 7 reports the complementary execution metrics. DA
completes a 30-day simulation in 12.32 seconds on average. In
end-to-end wall-clock time, this corresponds to an increase of
2.71 seconds over CE, 3.82 seconds over SD, and 5.85 seconds
over FD. In relative terms, DA is 28.2% slower than CE, 44.9%
slower than SD, and 90.4% slower than FD. This comparison
captures the practical cost of running DA in the simulator, but it
should not be interpreted as the cost of the adaptation decision
alone: fixed mechanisms differ in their own execution paths,
and FD is particularly lightweight.
The direct adaptation pipeline accounts for 13.87% of DA
wall-clock time, corresponding to about 1.71 seconds of the

DA reports 1.87 h average latency, 19.43% completion rate,
and 5.46 tasks/h, compared with 1.83 h, 19.59%, and 5.50
tasks/h for SD. CE obtains the highest completion rate and
throughput, whereas FD reports the lowest average latency
but the lowest completion rate.
7. Discussion
7.1. Dynamic Adaptation
The choice of a specific coordination mechanism to maxi-
mize a MAS performance objective is context-dependent, and
no single policy is asilver bulletacross all workloads, fault
rates, and malicious-agent configurations. Faults and commu-
nication issues are stochastic, as in real-life settings, and when
operating conditions evolve, different coordination mechanisms
may become more or less suitable for the current situation.
Results show that DA provides a competitive runtime strat-
egy, but it does not uniformly dominate all fixed coordination
mechanisms across the entire experimental space. In the aggre-
gated RQ1 results, DA reduces average latency relative to CE
and FD, but SD remains slightly better across average and tail
latency. This suggests that the current adaptation policy is able
to react to monitored execution conditions, but that its thresh-
olds and switching logic still leave room for calibration if the
goal is to consistently outperform the best fixed mechanism.
This result is important from a practical perspective. DA
should not be interpreted as a replacement for careful coordi-
nation design, but as a runtime control layer that can adjust co-
ordination decisions when the operating context changes. RQ2
further confirms this point: the temporal structure of fault win-
dows has a strong impact on DA performance. Profiles with
explicit recovery periods improve task completion and reduce
pending tasks, while malicious agents surges substantially in-
crease latency and tail latency. Thus, the effectiveness of adap-
tation depends not only on the selected thresholds, but also on
how faults, workload, communication degradation, and recov-
ery periods are distributed over time.
Overall, DA makes the coordination process more respon-
sive, but its behavior is sensitive to the monitored indicators
and to the policy used to map those indicators to coordination
mechanisms. Since these indicators are specified at the level
of the RTLolamonitor and the adaptation policy,COORDINATE
2.0can be recalibrated for different performance objectives. In
the motivating scenario, the main objective is to reduce task la-
tency; in other domains, the same architecture could prioritize
completion rate, pending tasks, robustness to malicious behav-
ior, or communication efficiency.
7.2. Adaptation Overhead
The RQ3 results show that the runtime cost introduced by DA
is mainly due to the monitoring query executed at each adapta-
tion check. Across all DA runs, one adaptation check requires
0.86 ms on average. The largest component is the interaction
with the RTLolamonitor, which requires 0.82 ms on average,
while the learning and scoring step requires only 0.03 ms. This
indicates that the online decision logic in the learning layer in-
curs negligible cost relative to the monitor layer.
However, these values should be interpreted in light of the ac-
celerated simulation setting. In our experiments,COORDINATE
2.0compresses long periods of simulated MAS execution,
such as days or even months, into less than one minute of wall-
clock time on average when DA is enabled. As a result, the

monitor processes many simulation events in a short time, in-
stead of receiving them gradually as it would in a real deploy-
ment. In a real deployment, the same monitoring and adapta-
tion logic would operate over physical time: events would be
distributed over seconds, minutes, and hours, and adaptation
checks would occur at configured intervals rather than being
compressed by the simulator. Under such conditions, a sub-
millisecond monitoring and decision cost would be expected
to have a substantially smaller impact on the system execution
timeline. Therefore, the overhead can be read as a conservative
estimate of the monitoring and adaptation pipeline.
7.3. On the Usage of the Tool
The RQ4 results provide evidence for the practical use of
COORDINATE 2.0as an experimental environment rather than
solely as a simulation engine. From a practitioner’s perspec-
tive, scalability is not determined solely by whether a run ter-
minates, but by whether repeated simulations can be executed,
compared, transferred across machines, and inspected after ex-
ecution. Results further show that this workflow does not
depend on workstation-class resources, makingCOORDINATE
2.0practical in more accessible experimental settings.
Another important aspect is the size of the simulation trace
generated byCOORDINATE 2.0. This aspect is central to the as-
sessment because traces contain the information produced dur-
ing the simulation, including task execution, failures, coordi-
nation decisions, monitoring states, and adaptation switches.
Therefore, traces must remain reasonably small: if they be-
came too large, storing, transferring, and inspecting them would
become impractical. However, the CSV traces generated with
COORDINATE 2.0, remain small even in the most demanding
available setting, with DA producing 11.52 MB. This shows
that the additional monitoring and adaptation evidence does not
compromise storage, transfer, orpost-mortemanalysis.
7.4. Threats to Validity
External validity.Threats to external validity concern possi-
ble biases in the design and interpretation of our simulation ex-
periments. Results may be influenced by the stochastic genera-
tion of items and deceptive messages, as well as by the chosen
probabilities of agent failure. We mitigate these threats through
an extensive experimental campaign involving 432 subjects and
4320 runs (10 runs per configuration), assessing significance
using Mann–Whitney U tests at the 95% confidence level. All
experiments are reproducible inCOORDINATE 2.02, and the re-
sults of the experiments are archived on Zenodo [6].
Internal validity.Threats to internal validity concern whether
the observed effects can be attributed to the switching policies
implemented by the Dynamic Adaptation strategy (i.e., how co-
ordination mechanisms are selected based on the monitored la-
tency values). Our MAS performance analysis relies on spe-
cific numerical settings for workload, failure probability, and
fraction of malicious agents, chosen to expose performance dif-
ferences among coordination mechanisms under these policies.
2https://github.com/IvanComp/COORDINATE-2.0
We mitigate potential confounding factors by varying one di-
mension at a time while keeping all other parameters constant.
However, selecting appropriate numerical input parameters re-
mains an open challenge in software performance studies [3].
Construct validity.Threats to construct validity concern
whether the selected metrics accurately capture the intended
performance objectives. We mainly use relative task latency
as the primary indicator because timely task completion is cen-
tral in the hospital scenario. However, latency alone does not
fully characterize system behavior under severe faults, since it
is computed over delivered tasks. For this reason, we comple-
ment latency with task completion rate, expired tasks, and task
throughput. These metrics are derived from timestamped simu-
lation events, reducing ambiguity in their computation.
Conclusion validity.Threats to conclusion validity concern
whether the results support the claims drawn from the experi-
ments. Since the simulations include stochastic events, individ-
ual runs may vary because of task generation, failures, message
losses, and malicious behavior. However, some performance
differences are small and may depend on the selected parameter
ranges. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as evidence
that Dynamic Adaptation can improve performance in the eval-
uated settings, rather than as proof that it always outperforms
fixed coordination mechanisms.
8. Conclusion
This paper addresses a limitation in fault-tolerant MAS: the
reliance on a fixed coordination mechanism that may become
ineffective when workload, failures, communication reliability,
or malicious behavior change during execution. To overcome
this limitation, we propose a runtime Dynamic Adaptation strat-
egy that combines stream-based monitoring and online learn-
ing. RTLolamonitors the MAS performance objective from
simulation events data stream, while a contextual bandit uses
this feedback and the current operating context to select among
three coordination mechanisms, aiming to improve MAS per-
formance over time.
We implement Dynamic Adaptation inCOORDINATE 2.0,
which we provide to researchers and practitioners as a discrete-
event simulation tool for configuring, executing, and inspect-
ing fault-tolerant MAS scenarios. The tool supports cus-
tom topologies, task generation processes, agent populations,
fault probabilities, communication failures, malicious behav-
iors, and dynamic fault windows. We evaluated the approach in
a smart-hospital scenario across 544 configurations and 5,440
simulation runs, varying the simulation input parameters to
cover diverse operating conditions. Results show that Dy-
namic Adaptation can improve the monitored objective rela-
tive to fixed coordination mechanisms with negligible over-
head, whileCOORDINATE 2.0scales to demanding campaigns
on both workstation and commodity hardware.
As future work, we plan to extend the framework with addi-
tional coordination mechanisms, validate the approach across
additional domains, and explore integrating the DA strategy
into real-world settings.
